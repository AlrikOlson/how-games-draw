PBR & the BRDF

A BRDF is the rule that says how much light bounces off a surface toward your eye, given where the light came from. Nearly every engine uses the same rule: a soft matte part plus a sharp highlight built from three terms called D, G and F, and it fixes the old problem of highlights that were as bright as an artist typed no matter how wide they were. The thing people get wrong: "PBR" is a set of rules about not inventing light, not one shader. Two engines can both be physically based and disagree on every constant; what they share is that a narrower highlight is a brighter one, and no surface gives back more light than it gets.

The lobe: where the light goes

Drag the light and watch how much bounces off in each direction.

Light hits a point on a surface and leaves in many directions, but not evenly. The lobe is the shape of that spread. A matte surface sends light everywhere about the same; a shiny one sends most of it in one tight beam near the mirror direction. Roughness sets how tight the beam is.

The readout under the canvas gives the light angle, the alpha value (roughness squared) and F0, plus how tall the peak is and where it lands.

The real version

The BRDF (bidirectional reflectance distribution function) answers one question: for light arriving from direction l, what fraction leaves toward the eye direction v, per unit of solid angle, which is a patch of directions the way an area is a patch of surface. The matte part is Lambert diffuse, albedo / π, the same in every direction. Albedo is the base colour: the share of light the surface does not soak up.

The highlight is the product of three terms, and each one is a probability rather than a colour. D is how many of the tiny mirrors (microfacets) point along the half vector h, the direction halfway between light and eye. G is how many of those are neither in a neighbour's shade nor hidden from view. F is the fraction of light a single facet mirrors instead of letting through. The 4 (n·l)(n·v) underneath converts from one facet's frame to the whole surface.

In words: light out is the matte part plus the highlight part, times how much light lands, which is n·l.

L_o = ∫ f(l, v) · L_i · (n·l) dω // one light: L_o = f · E · (n·l) f(l, v) = k_d · albedo / π + D(h) · G(l, v) · F(v·h) / (4 (n·l)(n·v)) h = normalize(l + v) α = roughness² F0 = mix(0.04, albedo, metallic) k_d = (1 − F) · (1 − metallic)

L_o is the light leaving, L_i the light arriving, n the surface normal (the direction the surface faces), α the roughness squared, F0 the head-on reflectance, and k_d the share left for the matte part after the highlight takes its cut.

What this code does: builds h, computes the three terms, and returns matte plus highlight scaled by n·l.

// Cook-Torrance specular + Lambert diffuse, one light, Unreal/Filament style
vec3 brdf(vec3 n, vec3 v, vec3 l, vec3 albedo, float rough, float metal) {
  vec3  h   = normalize(l + v);
  float NoL = max(dot(n, l), 0.0), NoV = max(dot(n, v), 1e-4);
  float NoH = max(dot(n, h), 0.0), VoH = max(dot(v, h), 0.0);
  float a   = rough * rough;                       // artist roughness -> GGX alpha
  vec3  F0  = mix(vec3(0.04), albedo, metal);      // metal: F0 is the base colour
  float D   = D_GGX(NoH, a);
  float G   = G_Smith(NoV, NoL, a);
  vec3  F   = F_Schlick(VoH, F0);
  vec3  spec = D * G * F / (4.0 * NoL * NoV + 1e-4);
  vec3  kd   = (1.0 - F) * (1.0 - metal);          // metal: no matte part at all
  return (kd * albedo / PI + spec) * NoL;          // caller multiplies by light radiance
}

A rougher surface spreads the highlight wider, and the wider it gets, the dimmer it must be.

Microfacets: the tiny mirrors

Bounce light rays off a 1 mm slice of bumpy surface and count which ones escape.

Zoom far enough into any surface and it is bumpy. Each bump acts like a tiny mirror tilted its own way. A rough surface has bumps tilted every which way, so the reflection scatters. Some bumps sit in the shade of their neighbours and some are hidden from your eye, and that lost light is what the D and G terms count.

What you're seeing

A 1 mm strip of surface as 96 flat mirrors with random tilts. Rays come in from the light handle on the left and bounce off the first facet they hit. Green facets get light; red ones face the light but sit in a neighbour's shade. A red ray segment bounced back into the surface and got stuck.

The histogram on the right counts facet tilts. The smooth curve over it is what the GGX formula predicts for that roughness.

  • Press "regenerate facets". The bumps change, the measured numbers move, but the Smith prediction stays put.

The real version

GGX is a statement about tilts, called slopes: most facets are nearly flat, but the tails are long, so a few facets point far off the average normal. That long tail is the soft halo around a highlight on a GGX surface. It is also why GGX replaced the older Beckmann and Blinn-Phong models, whose tails fall off like a bell curve and look clipped next to it.

Shadowing and masking are the same fact seen from two sides. Shadowing is a facet that faces the light while a neighbour blocks it. Masking is a facet that faces the eye while a bump hides it. Smith's G1 predicts the visible fraction from α and the angle alone, without knowing where the bumps are; the tiles compare that prediction with what this particular surface measures.

In words: D says how many facets point a given way; G1 says what share of them one direction can see; G multiplies the light's view by the eye's view.

D_GGX(h) = α² / (π ((n·h)² (α² − 1) + 1)²) // integrates to 1 over projected area p(slope) = 1 / (2α (1 + m²/α²)^(3/2)) // same distribution, 1D cross-section G1_Smith(x) = 2 (n·x) / ((n·x) + sqrt(α² + (1 − α²)(n·x)²)) // exact for GGX G1_Schlick(x) = (n·x) / ((n·x)(1 − k) + k) k = α/2 (IBL) or (r+1)²/8 (analytic lights, UE4) G(l, v) = G1(l) · G1(v) // separable, ignores height correlation

m is one facet's slope, x stands for either l or v, k is the constant Schlick's cheaper version of G1 uses, and IBL means lighting from a surrounding image rather than a single lamp.

What this code does: D_GGX is the tilt distribution; G_Smith is Filament's version with the 4 (n·l)(n·v) divider already folded in.

float D_GGX(float NoH, float a) {
  float a2 = a * a;
  float d  = NoH * NoH * (a2 - 1.0) + 1.0;
  return a2 / (PI * d * d);                // peak 1/(pi a^2): 0.32 at r=1, 3183 at r=0.1
}
float G_Smith(float NoV, float NoL, float a) {
  float a2 = a * a;
  float gv = NoL * sqrt(NoV * NoV * (1.0 - a2) + a2);
  float gl = NoV * sqrt(NoL * NoL * (1.0 - a2) + a2);
  return 0.5 / (gv + gl);                  // Filament's form: G / (4 NoL NoV) folded in
}

D counts which way the bumps point; G counts how many of them the light and the eye can both reach.

Fresnel: every surface mirrors at a slant

Drag the eye lower and watch a plain floor turn into a mirror.

F(θ) = F0 + (1 − F0)(1 − cos θ)^5

Look straight down at a puddle and you see the bottom. Look at it from far away, at a low angle, and it turns into a mirror. Every surface does this, plastic and metal alike; only the head-on amount differs. That head-on amount is F0, and it is the one number that separates metals from everything else.

What you're seeing

Top: how reflective a surface is, from 0 to 1, against the angle you view it from, for three F0 values: plastic, iron and silver. Bottom: a flat floor seen from the eye you can drag. Each reflected ray is drawn at the brightness the formula gives for that angle, so they fade when you look straight down and brighten as you get low.

Try this

  • Drag it straight above the floor. The plastic tile drops to about 0.04, which is why a dry floor looks dull from above.
  • Compare iron and silver at any angle: metals start high and have less far to climb.

The real version

F0 is the reflectance looking straight down. For almost every dielectric, which is any non-metal such as plastic, wood or skin, it sits between 0.02 and 0.05, so engines hard-code 0.04 and give artists no control over it. Metals have a coloured F0 of 0.5 to 0.98, and that colour is what the base colour texture stores in the metallic workflow.

The curve matters more than the constant. Every material, plastic or silver, reaches 100% reflectance as the view angle approaches 90°. A wet road at dusk mirrors the headlights ahead for this reason, and a shader that skips Fresnel makes floors look dry and dull at a distance. Schlick's formula is a cheap fit to the real Fresnel equations that costs one pow, and every engine in the table below uses it.

In words: start at F0 and climb to 1 as the view goes flat; the fifth power keeps the value near F0 until the angle gets steep.

F_Schlick(v·h) = F0 + (1 − F0) · (1 − v·h)^5 F0 = ((n − 1) / (n + 1))² // from the index of refraction: glass n=1.5 gives 0.04 F0 = mix(vec3(0.04), baseColor, metallic)

θ in the caption is the angle between the view direction and the surface normal; v·h is its cosine. n on the second line is the index of refraction, how strongly the material bends light. The third line is how the metallic workflow picks F0.

What this code does: one pow and one mix; the UE4 line gets the same curve from an exp2 for two fewer instructions.

vec3 F_Schlick(float VoH, vec3 F0) {
  float f = pow(1.0 - VoH, 5.0);
  return F0 + (1.0 - F0) * f;               // == mix(F0, vec3(1.0), f)
}
// UE4 replaces pow with a spherical-gaussian fit, same shape, 2 fewer instructions:
float f = exp2((-5.55473 * VoH - 6.98316) * VoH);

Everything reflects at a low angle; F0 only sets where the curve starts.

The real version

The area under the lobe is fixed because D integrates to one, so a wider lobe must be a lower one. Blinn-Phong has no such rule: its peak is whatever ks the artist typed, and at roughness 1 its exponent hits zero and the whole sphere glows. The normalisation checkbox adds the (p+8)/8π factor that fixes this, the first step most engines took toward PBR around 2010, before switching to GGX.

At metallic 1 the diffuse lobe disappears. The sphere is lit only by its highlight, and that highlight takes on the base colour, because for a metal F0 is the base colour. This is the whole metal/roughness workflow: one RGB texture that means "albedo" for non-metals and "highlight colour" for metals, one channel to choose between them, one channel for roughness. The engine never stores a separate specular colour.

In words: light out is matte plus highlight, times the light landing, times n·l, plus a flat ambient guess; the light landing falls off with distance squared.

radiance = (kd · albedo/π + D·G·F / (4 (n·l)(n·v))) · E · (n·l) + ambient · albedo E = intensity / d² // point light, inverse square, d in metres Blinn-Phong: spec = F0 · (n·h)^p, p = 2/α² − 2 // exponent matching GGX width (Karis 2013) normalised: spec · (p + 8) / (8π)

E is irradiance, the light power landing per square metre. d is the distance to the light in metres. p is the Blinn-Phong exponent, chosen so its highlight is as wide as the GGX one.

What this code does: finds the light direction and distance, works out the light landing at P, calls the BRDF, adds ambient, then squashes the range to fit the screen.

// one point light, metal/roughness inputs, as in a deferred lighting pass
vec3 L    = lightPos - P;
float d2  = dot(L, L);  L /= sqrt(d2);
vec3 E    = lightColor * lightIntensity / d2;          // irradiance at P, W/m^2
vec3 col  = brdf(N, V, L, baseColor, roughness, metallic) * E;
col      += ambient * baseColor;                       // placeholder for the IBL below
col       = col / (col + 1.0);                         // tone map, then sRGB encode
fragColor = vec4(pow(col, vec3(1.0 / 2.2)), 1.0);

Physically based shading buys you one thing: move any slider and the surface still looks like a real material.

Sky light, baked: the split sum

Hover the lookup table and slide roughness to see how two baked textures stand in for thousands of samples.

A single lamp is easy: one direction, one bounce. The real world lights a surface from every direction at once, and adding all of them up per pixel would take thousands of samples. The split sum trick bakes two tables ahead of time so the shader does two texture reads instead.

What you're seeing

Left: a one-line environment (sky, sun, ground) blurred at six roughness levels, one per mip, which is a smaller pre-blurred copy of a texture. A mirror surface reads the sharp top strip; a rough one reads a blurry lower strip.

Right: the 64×64 lookup table (LUT), computed here with 128 samples per texel. Red is the weight on F0, green is the constant that gets added on top.

  • Hover the LUT. Both sliders follow the cursor and the tiles show scale, bias and the result for F0 = 0.04.

The real version

A point light is one term of the rendering integral, the sum over every incoming direction. An environment is all of the terms at once, and integrating the BRDF against a cubemap (six images that wrap around the scene) per pixel is thousands of samples. Karis's split sum pulls the integral apart into two factors that can each be baked.

The first factor is the environment blurred by the GGX lobe, stored once per roughness in the mip chain of a cubemap, so a lookup is one textureLod. The second is the BRDF integrated against a plain white environment. It depends only on n·v and roughness and is the same for every scene, so it ships with the engine as a 2D texture.

The LUT stores two numbers because F0 factors out linearly: the red channel is the weight on F0, the green channel is the constant that remains. Green is the reason a black dielectric still shows a rim of sky at grazing angles. The blur step assumes n = v = r, so highlights from the environment come out round instead of stretched at grazing angles, the one visible artifact of the split sum.

In words: the full sum is roughly the blurred environment in the mirror direction, times F0 × scale + bias.

∫ L_i(l) f(l,v) (n·l) dl ≈ [ ∫ L_i(l) D(h) (n·l) dl ] · [ ∫ f(l,v) (n·l) dl ] prefiltered(R, roughness) · (F0 · scale + bias) mip = roughness · (mipCount − 1) // this page: 6 levels, r = 0, 0.2, ... 1

R is the mirror direction of the eye, scale and bias are the LUT's red and green channels, and mip picks which blur level to read.

What this code does: one read from the blurred cubemap, one from the LUT, then a matte term from a separate blurred map, all scaled by ambient occlusion.

// ambient specular from a prefiltered cubemap + 2D LUT, 2 texture reads
vec3  R      = reflect(-V, N);
float NoV    = max(dot(N, V), 0.0);
vec3  pref   = textureLod(prefilteredEnv, R, roughness * MAX_MIP).rgb;
vec2  ab     = texture(brdfLUT, vec2(NoV, roughness)).rg;   // scale, bias
vec3  spec   = pref * (F0 * ab.x + ab.y);
vec3  diff   = texture(irradianceEnv, N).rgb * baseColor * (1.0 - metallic);
vec3  ambient = (diff + spec) * ao;

Two texture reads stand in for thousands of samples, at the cost of round highlights at grazing angles.

Tradeoffs: which model, and what it costs

The instruction counts are rough estimates for one light in a deferred pass, where lighting runs once per screen pixel after geometry is drawn. The shape of the tail and the number of inputs matter more than the exact count.

At high roughness the standard model loses light. Single-bounce GGX with Smith throws away the light that bounces between facets, up to about 30% at roughness 1 for a white metal. Filament, Frostbite and Unreal each add a correction term that scales the highlight by 1 plus F0 times the LUT's missing energy.

Two names, same data. "Specular workflow" stores a matte colour and an RGB highlight colour (F0) as two textures; "metallic workflow" derives F0 from one base colour and a single metallic number.
