Shadow Maps & Cascades

A shadow map is a photo of the scene taken from where the light sits, except each pixel stores distance instead of colour. Games use it because it turns "is this pixel in shadow" into one texture read per pixel. That read compares a smooth surface against a grid of stored distances, and every artifact on this page comes from that mismatch. The thing people get wrong: "shadow bias" is not one number. Acne, peter-panning and grazing-angle leaks are three different failures with three different fixes.

Two passes: write depth, then compare

A lantern lights a village square at night and the player stands behind a tree. Is the player in shadow? Drag the lantern, its aim handle or the player, and hover the ground to follow one pixel through the check.

hover the ground

The light takes a distance photo of the scene first. Then, for every pixel the camera sees, the shader checks whether something closer to the light shows up in that photo at the same spot. If so, the pixel is in shadow. That is the whole trick, and it costs one texture read per pixel.

The real version

Pass 1 renders the scene from the lantern with no colour at all. Each texel, one cell of the map, keeps the depth of the nearest surface along its ray, where depth means distance from the light measured along the light's forward direction. In this top-down world the map is a single row; in a game it is a full 2D texture.

Pass 2 renders from the camera. For each ground pixel, transform its position into light space using the light's view-projection matrix, which gives a texture coordinate u and a depth z. Read the texel at u and compare: a stored depth smaller than z means something sits in front, so the pixel is shadowed. There is no ray cast and no geometry lookup, only one texture fetch.

In words: measure how far the point is from the light, find the cell it lands in, and compare against the distance stored there.

d = p − L z = d · fwd // depth from the light x = d · right // lateral offset u = 0.5 + 0.5 · (x / z) / tan(fov / 2) i = floor(u · N) shadowed = z > shadowMap[i]

p is the ground point, L the light position, fwd and right the light's forward and sideways directions, fov its 60° cone, N the texel count and i the cell index.

What this code does: pass 1 writes depth from the light's viewpoint; pass 2 projects each pixel into the light's view, reads one texel and compares.

// pass 1, from the light: depth-only draw, no color target
gl_Position = uLightProj * uLightView * vec4(position, 1.0);

// pass 2, per pixel: project into the light and compare
vec4 lp  = uLightViewProj * vec4(worldPos, 1.0);
vec3 ndc = lp.xyz / lp.w;                    // perspective divide
vec2 uv  = ndc.xy * 0.5 + 0.5;               // [-1,1] to texture space
float zLit = ndc.z * 0.5 + 0.5;               // this pixel's depth from the light
float zMap = texture(uShadowMap, uv).r;       // nearest surface the light saw
float shadow = zLit > zMap + uBias ? 0.0 : 1.0;

A shadow map is a distance photo from the light; shading a pixel is one lookup and one compare.

Resolution: one cell covers many pixels

The lantern shines past a house onto the long wall at the far side of the square. Drag the lantern away from the wall, or drag the wall's ends so the light grazes it, and watch each cell of the photo grow.

Each cell of the shadow map covers a wedge of space that gets wider the farther it travels from the light. Far from the light, one stored distance ends up spread over many screen pixels, so shadow edges go blocky. Surfaces that lean away from the light make it worse: a cell lands on a long strip of surface instead of a short one.

The real version

A texel is a wedge of light directions. Its width in metres grows with depth from the light, so a far receiver gets one depth value spread over many screen pixels. That stretch is called perspective aliasing, and it is why the shadow edge behind the house gets blockier as the lantern moves away.

Projective aliasing is the other stretch. When a surface tilts away from the light, a texel of width w lands on a strip of surface w / cos θ long, where θ is the angle between the surface normal (the direction the surface faces) and the direction to the light. At 80° that strip is six times the texel width. The wall stripes show it directly.

In words: cell width grows with distance, then stretches again by how much the surface leans away.

w_texel(z) = 2 · z · tan(fov/2) / N // metres per texel at depth z footprint on surface = w_texel / cos θ px per texel = footprint · screen px per metre

z is depth from the light, fov the 60° cone, N the texel count and θ the angle between the surface normal and the light direction.

Shadow-map cells grow with distance and stretch on tilted surfaces, and every blocky shadow edge comes from that.

Bias: acne, peter-panning, and the fixes

A stone wall in the square both blocks the lantern and gets lit by it. Tilt the wall so the light grazes its face, switch bias modes, and hover the face for the numbers.

hover the wall face

A surface can shadow itself by mistake. The map stores one distance per cell, but a surface point half a cell to the side is slightly farther away, so it fails its own test and gets a stripe of false shadow: that is acne. The fix is a small bias, a slack added to the compare, but push it too far and shadows float away from their objects, which is peter-panning.

What you're seeing

The stone wall blocks the lantern and gets lit by it. Its lit face is drawn as a band: pale amber where the depth test says lit, red where the face wrongly shadows itself. The dotted white lines through the wall's ends are where the true shadow edge should be; when the dark ground starts past them, an amber arrow measures how far the shadow has floated off the wall.

The three tiles report how much of the face is in acne, how far the shadow has detached from the wall, and the angle between the face and the lantern.

The real version

Each texel stores the depth at its center ray. A face point half a texel to one side is a little farther from the light than that stored value, so with zero bias it fails its own test. The stripe period is exactly one texel. Depth precision is not the cause; a 32-bit float map shows the same stripes.

A constant bias pushes every comparison back by c metres. Large enough to cover the grazing-angle case, it also pushes the shadow away from the wall's back face: peter-panning. Slope-scaled bias grows the offset with tan θ, so flat-on faces get almost none and grazing faces get a lot.

Normal offset moves the lookup position off the surface along its normal instead of changing the depth. That is why it survives grazing angles where depth bias alone needs a huge value.

In words: acne is the test failing by less than one texel's worth of depth; each fix adds slack, either to the depth or to the lookup position.

acne: z_p > map[i] // same face, half a texel on constant: z_p > map[i] + c slope: z_p > map[i] + c + s · w_texel · tan θ normal: p' = p + n · k · w_texel z_p' > map[i(p')] + c

z_p is the point's depth from the light, map[i] the stored depth, c the constant term in metres, s the slope scale, w_texel the texel width at that depth, θ the grazing angle, n the surface normal and k the normal offset in texels.

What this code does: works out the texel size and the surface tilt, nudges the lookup point off the surface, then adds a tilt-scaled slack to the compare.

// receiver-side corrections; set a term to 0.0 to drop it
float texelWorld = 2.0 * zLight * tan(uHalfFov) / uMapSize;
float cosT = clamp(dot(N, L), 0.05, 1.0);
float tanT = sqrt(1.0 - cosT * cosT) / cosT;

vec3 pOff = worldPos + N * (uNormalK * texelWorld); // normal offset
vec4 lp   = uLightViewProj * vec4(pOff, 1.0);
vec3 ndc  = lp.xyz / lp.w;
float bias = uConst + uSlope * texelWorld * tanT; // slope-scaled
float zMap = texture(uShadowMap, ndc.xy * 0.5 + 0.5).r;
float lit  = (ndc.z * 0.5 + 0.5) > zMap + bias ? 0.0 : 1.0;

Acne and peter-panning are the two ends of one slider; slope-scaled and normal-offset bias let you avoid both.

Soft edges: PCF, PCSS and variance maps

The player stands right at the edge of a tree's shadow in the lantern light. Hover a shadowed spot to see which cells the filter reads, on the strip and in the scene, then switch filter modes and watch the edge under the player go soft.

hover the ground

A single lookup gives a hard, jagged edge. The fix is to read several neighbouring cells, test each one, and average the yes/no answers, which blurs the edge. Fancier versions size the blur to match the geometry, so a shadow is sharp where the object touches the ground and softer farther away, like a real one.

What you're seeing

In the scene the same band is drawn as a blue fan from the lantern.

The real version

Percentage-closer filtering, PCF, does the depth test at several neighbouring texels and averages the results. Averaging the depths first and testing once would be wrong: the mean of two surfaces is a surface that does not exist. The averaged verdict is a coverage fraction, so the edge blurs over the kernel width, the span of texels the filter reads. The blur is the same width everywhere, which real shadows are not.

PCSS, percentage-closer soft shadows, makes the kernel depend on geometry. First a blocker search averages the depths of the map texels that are closer than the receiver, the surface being shaded. That average blocker depth and the light's size give a penumbra width, the width of the soft edge, by similar triangles, and the PCF kernel is sized to it. The shadow is sharp where the receiver touches the tree trunk and widens with distance.

Variance shadow maps, VSM, store depth and depth squared, blur the map itself once, and bound the lit fraction with Chebyshev's inequality, a rule that turns a mean and a variance into a worst-case probability. One bilinear fetch gives a soft edge. The cost is light bleeding: where two occluders at different depths land in one blurred texel, the variance is large, the bound is loose, and light leaks into the shadow behind the far one.

In words: PCF averages several yes/no tests; PCSS first measures how far away the blocker is to pick the blur width; VSM blurs the map once and turns mean and spread into a lit probability.

PCF: lit = (1/n) · Σ [ z_p ≤ map[i + k] ] PCSS: d_b = mean of map[j] where map[j] < z_p w_pen = (z_p − d_b) / d_b · w_light kernel = w_pen / w_texel(z_p) texels VSM: μ = E[z] σ² = E[z²] − μ² p_lit = σ² / (σ² + (z_p − μ)²) if z_p > μ p_lit = 1 otherwise

n is the tap count, k the tap offset, z_p the receiver depth, d_b the average blocker depth, w_light the light's size, and μ and σ² the mean and variance of the blurred map.

What this code does: the PCF loop reads a 5×5 block and averages the compares; the PCSS part searches for blockers, sizes the penumbra, then runs a PCF of that size.

// PCF, 5x5 box; sampler2DShadow compares 2x2 per fetch
float pcf = 0.0;
for (int y = -2; y <= 2; y++)
for (int x = -2; x <= 2; x++)
  pcf += texture(uShadowCmp, vec3(uv + vec2(x, y) * uTexel, zLit - uBias));
pcf /= 25.0;

// PCSS: the blocker search sizes the kernel
float searchR = uLightSize * (zLit - uNear) / zLit;
float dBlocker = 0.0; int n = 0;
for (int k = 0; k < 16; k++) {
  float d = texture(uShadowMap, uv + poisson[k] * searchR).r;
  if (d < zLit) { dBlocker += d; n++; }
}
if (n == 0) return 1.0;        // no blocker: lit, skip the filter
dBlocker /= float(n);
float penumbra = (zLit - dBlocker) / dBlocker * uLightSize;
return pcfPoisson(uv, zLit, penumbra * uNear / zLit);

Average the test results, never the depths; PCSS sizes the blur from the blocker distance, and VSM pre-blurs the map but pays in light bleeding.

Cascades: one map per slice of view

Morning sun over the same village, seen from the side, with the camera floating behind the player. Drag the camera or the sun to see how the view gets split into near, mid and far slices, and how big each slice's shadow cells are.

hover the ground

The sun lights the whole world at once, so one shadow map for the sun has to cover hundreds of metres, and its cells end up 15 cm wide at your feet. Cascades fix that by splitting the view into slices by distance and giving each slice its own map. The near slice gets small cells; the far slice gets big ones nobody looks at closely.

The real version

A sun has no position, only a direction, so its shadow map is an orthographic box, a projection with no perspective where every ray is parallel, wrapped around the camera frustum. One 2048² map over a 300 m view distance gives 15 cm texels at the player's feet. Cascades split the view depth into slices and fit a map to each, and the coloured stripe under the ground shows which cascade each point samples from.

The split scheme decides where texel size jumps. Uniform splits waste the near map. Logarithmic splits match the perspective texel growth exactly but leave the far cascades huge. The practical split blends the two with λ, a weight from 0 (uniform) to 1 (logarithmic).

At each boundary the texel size steps by the ratio shown. That seam is visible in a shipped game unless the boundary is dithered or the two cascades are blended over a metre or two.

In words: pick the split depths as a blend of even spacing and doubling spacing, then wrap a box around each slice and divide its width by 2048.

z_i = λ · n · (f/n)^(i/N) + (1 − λ) · (n + (f − n) · i/N) bounds_i = min/max of slice corners on (r, d) texel_i = width_i / 2048 // metres, per cascade

n and f are the near and far planes, N the cascade count, i the split index, λ the blend weight, and r and d the axes across and along the sun's rays.

What this code does: finds the first cascade whose split depth is past this pixel, projects into that cascade's box, and runs PCF with a bias scaled to that cascade's texel.

// cascade selection by view depth; uSplits holds z_1..z_N
int c = 0;
for (int i = 0; i < NUM_CASCADES - 1; i++)
  if (viewZ > uSplits[i]) c = i + 1;

vec4 lp = uCascadeViewProj[c] * vec4(worldPos, 1.0); // ortho: w = 1
vec3 uvz = lp.xyz * 0.5 + 0.5;
float shadow = pcf(uShadowArray, vec3(uvz.xy, float(c)), uvz.z - uBias[c]);
// uBias[c] scales with the cascade's texel size

Cascades give the sun several maps at once, sharp near the camera and coarse far away, at the price of visible seams.

The perspective spotlight map does not, and that is where 24 or 32 bits earn their cost.

The trap: shadow bias is three corrections, not one slider. Constant bias covers depth rounding within a flat-on texel, slope-scaled bias covers the depth spread across a tilted texel, and normal offset moves the lookup off the surface so the tilted case needs no large depth offset at all. An engine that exposes a single "shadow bias" is usually setting the constant term and hiding the other two per cascade.
