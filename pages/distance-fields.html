Distance Field Explorer

A distance field is a grid laid over the level where every cell stores one number: how far it is to the nearest wall, with a minus sign when the cell is inside one. That single number lets a ray jump through empty space, gives soft shadows without extra rays, and keeps an edge crisp at low resolution. The thing people mix up is sparse versus signed. Signed says what the number means; sparse says where you bother to store it. Game engines use both at once.

How far to the nearest wall

A player crosses a dungeon room. Drag them anywhere and the field says how far they can move before touching the pillar, the table or the wall. Rings are drawn every 0.5 m.

d = —white circle = how far the player can move before touching anything

Think of the room as graph paper. Every square holds one number: how far to the nearest wall. A minus sign means the square is inside something solid. That is the whole data structure, and everything else on this page is a way of using that one number.

What you're seeing

The room from above. The round stone pillar, the wooden table and the low wall are the three solid things in it. Amber floor is outside them, blue is inside, and the thin white line is the surface, where the distance is zero. Colour fades as the distance grows, with a brighter ring every 0.5 m so you can count distance by eye.

The blue figure is the player. The white circle around them is the stored distance: nothing solid is inside it, so the player can move that far in any direction without touching anything. The dashed line points at the nearest surface, and the inset shows what the player sees looking that way.

Try this

  • Drag the player onto open floor between the pillar and the table. The circle grows until it just touches the nearer one and never crosses it.
  • Drag the player onto the table. The readout flips to a minus sign, and the circle now shows how deep in they are.
  • Slide the player across the low wall. The number passes through zero smoothly, with no jump.

The real version

The grid is a texture, and each cell of it is a texel, one stored sample. A distance field texel stores the distance to the closest surface in world units, here metres. A signed distance field, or SDF, also stores the sign: negative inside, positive outside. The inside/outside test comes free with the same read.

An ordinary raster texture stores "is there something here". A distance field stores "how far is the nearest something", a much richer answer from the same memory. That answer is the guarantee the white circle draws: nothing solid is inside that radius. A sliding collision uses it directly: move the player, read d, and if d went negative push them back out by that much. Ray marching, soft shadows and ambient occlusion in the sections below rest on the same guarantee.

Because the value changes smoothly from cell to cell, a low-resolution field still reconstructs a crisp edge under bilinear filtering, the GPU's default way of blending the four nearest texels when you read between them. That is why a 64×64 SDF renders sharp UI text at any zoom. Valve published the trick in 2007 and most game UI uses it today.

One number per cell, the distance to the nearest surface, with a sign that says inside or outside.

Store the whole room, or only near the walls

Sparse

A full grid stores a distance for every cell of the room, even in the open middle far from anything. Most of those far-away numbers are never used, so a sparse field cuts the grid into small square bricks and keeps only the bricks that touch a surface. Same room, a fraction of the memory.

What you're seeing

Left is the dense grid: every cell of the room stored, open floor included. Right is the sparse version. The faint grid lines are brick borders, the bright-outlined bricks are the ones kept, and the dimmed bricks show plain floor because nothing was stored there. The band width slider sets how far from a surface a brick can be and still be kept.

Try this

  • Set resolution to 128. The dense byte count quadruples; the sparse count grows much less, because only the ring of bricks around the pillar, table and wall grows.
  • Drop the band to 0.1 m. The kept bricks shrink to a thin shell around each object.
  • Switch brick size to 16. Fewer, bigger bricks, and more wasted open-floor cells inside each kept brick.

The real version

Each cell holds a float16 value, a 16-bit number that takes 2 bytes, so every byte count in the table is the cell count times two. The 2D row is what you see above. The 3D row is computed, not guessed: the same pillar, table and wall as a sphere, a rotated box and a capsule, voxelized at N³, where a voxel is one cell of a 3D grid, and cut into N/brick bricks per axis.

Memory in a sparse field scales with surface area, because only bricks near a surface survive. Memory in a dense field scales with volume, because every cell survives. That is why the gap widens as resolution goes up.

Unreal Engine 5 keeps both its per-mesh distance fields and its global distance field as bricks in an atlas, one big texture holding many small bricks side by side, for exactly this reason. It also quantizes each voxel below float16, meaning it stores fewer bits per number, on top of that.

Keep the bricks near a surface and drop the rest; memory then grows with surface, not volume.

Walking a ray with the stored distances

The player fires a bolt across the room. Drag the player or the aim point and count the steps the ray takes to reach the first solid thing.

each circle is one step

The stored number is a promise: nothing is closer than this distance. So a ray can jump forward by exactly that much and never pass through a wall. In open floor it takes big strides; near the pillar or the table it takes small careful ones.

What you're seeing

The white line is the bolt's path, from the player toward the aim point. Each circle is one step: its radius is the distance the field reported there, and the ray moves to the edge of that circle. The amber dot is where the bolt lands and its label says what it hit. A ✕ in the step counter means the ray left the stored 10 m slice without hitting anything. The inset is the player's own view down the ray.

Try this

  • Drag the player into open floor. The first few circles get huge, and the ray covers most of the room in two or three steps.
  • Switch to sparse. Out on the open floor, where no brick was stored, the circles shrink to one band width and the step count climbs.
  • Switch to sparse + coarse. The big strides come back while the fine data stays sparse.

The real version

This is sphere tracing, also called ray marching an SDF. At each step the ray reads the distance d at its current position and advances by d. The rule that makes it safe is the one from the first section: no surface lies within radius d, so a step of d cannot cross anything. When d falls under a small threshold, call it a hit.

In words: read the distance, move that far, repeat until the distance is nearly zero.

With a dense field that works everywhere. With a sparse field the bricks over open floor were never stored, so out there the only safe answer is "at least one band width", and the ray crawls in steps of the band.

Engines fix this with a coarse fallback: a low-resolution dense field (16³ here, cells of 0.625 m) that answers the open-floor case with a conservative distance, one shortened enough to stay safe given the coarse cell size. Unreal's global distance field clipmaps and Godot's SDFGI cascades are this idea at scale, as nested fields around the camera, finer close in and coarser far out. The stored data stays small; the step count comes back down.

Step by the stored distance and you can never overshoot; a sparse field needs a coarse fallback to keep the steps big in open space.

Soft torch shadows from the same field

The player carries a torch through the room. Drag them and watch the shadows behind the pillar, the table and the wall go soft as they stretch away.

shade = min over the ray of k · d / t

Soft shadows come from the same numbers. Each floor pixel walks a ray toward the torch and remembers how close it came to brushing a surface. Just missing the pillar means a faint shadow; missing it by a lot means no shadow. No extra rays and no blur pass.

What you're seeing

The floor is lit by the torch in the player's hand; drag the player. Floor brightness is the shadow: dark where the ray to the torch hit something, amber where it passed clean, and a gradient where it passed close. The two labels track the pillar's shadow: sharp right behind the pillar, soft further along.

Try this

  • Drag the player close to the low wall. Its shadow has a sharp edge next to the wall and a soft one across the room.
  • Push k to 48. Edges harden across the whole floor.
  • Pull k to 2. Everything goes soft and smeared.

The real version

A shadow ray that hits a surface is fully dark. One that passes a surface at distance d after travelling t is partly dark, scaled by k·d/t. That ratio is the angle the occluder covers as seen from the floor point, so the penumbra widens with distance from the occluder without any extra rays. The constant k sets how quickly an edge goes soft.

In words: keep the smallest k·d/t seen along the ray; that is how lit the pixel is.

Raster shadow maps, which store depth as seen from the light, need filtering tricks to fake the same thing.

This is Unreal's Distance Field Shadows. The same march with a shorter ray and no light is Distance Field Ambient Occlusion, a measure of how boxed-in each point is. Lumen's software ray tracing traces these fields too, when hardware ray tracing is off.

The nearest miss along the shadow ray sets how dark the pixel is, and the distance to that miss sets how soft the edge is.
