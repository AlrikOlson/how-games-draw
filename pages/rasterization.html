Rasterization & Depth

Rasterization is the step where the GPU turns a triangle into the list of pixels it covers, and the depth buffer is the step that decides which triangle wins when several land on the same pixel. Both are cheap, and this page is about why they are cheap and where they break. The thing people get wrong: the depth buffer does not store distance from the camera. It stores z divided by w, a squashed value that spends most of its precision in the first metre, so depth bugs are almost always a near-plane problem, not a bit-count problem.

Three views of one triangle

A knight has pitched a tent at his camp. Drag a corner of the tent canvas in the top-down view and watch that one triangle travel to the player's screen.

Every triangle starts as three points in 3D. Before the GPU can colour a single pixel it has to work out where those points land on the screen. That takes three steps: measure each point from the camera, divide by its distance so far things come out small, then map the result to a pixel position. The divide is the whole of perspective.

What you're seeing

Left: the camp from above. The camera sits at the bottom and looks up the page, the dashed lines are the edges of what it can see, and the short white line is the near plane. The amber triangle is the tent canvas: two pegs on the ground and a peak 2.8 m up. The knight, the campfire, the trees and the farmhouse are there so you can find the same things in all three views.

Middle: the same camp after the divide, squeezed into the square the screen maps to. Anything outside the white square gets cut off. Right: what the player sees, the 1920×1080 screen with the tent placed in pixels.

The real version

The vertex shader, which is the small program that runs once per corner, multiplies each position by the projection matrix and outputs four numbers: x, y, z and w. For a perspective camera w equals the view-space depth, the distance along the direction the camera is pointing. Nothing is divided yet.

Clipping, which is cutting off the parts of a triangle the camera cannot see, happens in this space. The near-plane test is a simple comparison here, z ≥ −w, and the divide has not yet blown up points sitting right at the camera.

After clipping, the hardware divides x, y and z by w. The result is normalized device coordinates, or NDC, a cube running from −1 to +1 on each axis. The viewport transform then maps NDC x and y to pixels.

In words: build the projection, multiply, divide by w, then scale to 1920×1080 with y flipped so pixel 0 is at the top.

f = 1 / tan(fov_y / 2) A = −(far + near) / (far − near) B = −2 · far · near / (far − near) clip = P · view = [ f/aspect · x, f · y, A · z + B, −z ] ndc = clip.xyz / clip.w // w = −z_view = depth along the camera axis px = (ndc.x · 0.5 + 0.5) · 1920 py = (0.5 − ndc.y · 0.5) · 1080 // y flipped: D3D, Vulkan and Metal put pixel 0 at the top

fov_y is the vertical field of view, aspect is width over height, near and far are the closest and farthest distances the camera keeps, z is the view-space depth (negative in front of the camera), and px, py is the pixel position.

What this code does: the shader outputs the four-number position, then the hardware divides, scales to pixels and remaps z into the 0 to 1 range the depth buffer stores.

// vertex shader: no divide here, the hardware does it after clipping
out vec4 gl_Position = uProjection * uView * vec4(position, 1.0);

// fixed-function, per vertex, after clipping against z >= -w
vec3 ndc = clip.xyz / clip.w;                 // perspective divide
vec2 px  = (ndc.xy * vec2(0.5, -0.5) + 0.5) * vec2(1920.0, 1080.0);
float zb = ndc.z * 0.5 + 0.5;                // GL range [-1,1] -> depth buffer [0,1]

A vertex goes in as three numbers, comes out as four, and one divide by w turns it into a pixel position.

Which pixels a triangle covers

The knight's shield is two triangles drawn on a tiny 24×16 screen. Drag its corners and hover a pixel to see the three numbers that decide whether the shield covers it.

hover a pixel

A pixel is covered when its center point sits inside the triangle. The GPU checks that with three cheap tests, one per edge, each saying which side of the edge the point is on. When two triangles share an edge, a center sitting exactly on the line needs a tie-break rule, or it gets drawn twice or not at all. That rule is called top-left.

What you're seeing

A 24×16 screen, each pixel with a white dot at its center, and the knight's camp faint behind it. The shield is a kite: the left half is triangle A in amber, with its covered pixels tinted by how close they sit to each corner, and the right half is triangle B in blue. The two halves share the vertical seam down the middle, and ten pixel centers sit exactly on that seam.

A red pixel along the seam is one that both triangles claimed, or that neither did. The "on screen" inset is the shield as the player would see it: a gap where a pixel was missed, a bright pixel where it was drawn twice. The counts under the canvas track both cases.

Try this

  • Hover a pixel just inside the left edge of the shield. All three E values are positive. Hover the pixel next to it, outside, and one goes negative.
  • Drag the top corner one pixel sideways so the seam leans. Now no center sits exactly on it, and the tie rule stops mattering.
Shared-edge tie

The real version

An edge function is twice the signed area of the triangle formed by an edge and the test point. It is positive on one side of the edge, negative on the other, and zero on the line. A pixel center is inside when all three edge functions have the same sign.

Each edge function is linear in x and y, so walking along a row of pixels means adding a constant per step. There are no multiplies in the inner loop, which is a big part of why rasterization is cheap.

The zero case matters on a shared edge. If a center lands exactly on the line, both triangles would draw it, and a see-through pixel would be blended twice. The top-left rule breaks the tie: a pixel on an edge belongs to the triangle for which that edge is a top edge or a left edge.

The same three numbers divided by the triangle's area are the barycentric weights, which say how close the pixel is to each corner. They blend colours, texture coordinates and everything else across the triangle, and they come for free.

In words: compute the three edge values, require all three to be positive, and divide them by the area to get the blend weights.

E01(x, y) = (x − x0)(y1 − y0) − (y − y0)(x1 − x0) area = E01(x2, y2) // twice the signed area inside = E12 > 0 && E20 > 0 && E01 > 0 // or == 0 on a top or left edge λ0 = E12 / area λ1 = E20 / area λ2 = E01 / area attr = λ0·a0 + λ1·a1 + λ2·a2

x0, y0 through x2, y2 are the three corners, E01 is the edge function for the edge from corner 0 to corner 1, area is twice the triangle's area, λ0 to λ2 are the blend weights, and a0 to a2 are the values stored at each corner.

What this code does: walks every pixel in the triangle's bounding box, tests the three edges with the tie rule, and emits a fragment with its blend weights.

// one triangle, screen-space vertices v0 v1 v2 in pixels, y down
float edge(vec2 a, vec2 b, vec2 p) { return (p.x-a.x)*(b.y-a.y) - (p.y-a.y)*(b.x-a.x); }
bool topLeft(vec2 a, vec2 b) { vec2 d = b - a; return d.y > 0.0 || (d.y == 0.0 && d.x < 0.0); }

float area = edge(v0, v1, v2);           // swap v1,v2 first if negative
for (int y = minY; y <= maxY; y++)
for (int x = minX; x <= maxX; x++) {       // bounding-box walk
  vec2 p = vec2(x, y) + 0.5;               // pixel center
  float e0 = edge(v1, v2, p), e1 = edge(v2, v0, p), e2 = edge(v0, v1, p);
  bool in0 = e0 > 0.0 || (e0 == 0.0 && topLeft(v1, v2));
  bool in1 = e1 > 0.0 || (e1 == 0.0 && topLeft(v2, v0));
  bool in2 = e2 > 0.0 || (e2 == 0.0 && topLeft(v0, v1));
  if (in0 && in1 && in2) {
    vec3 bary = vec3(e0, e1, e2) / area;   // interpolation weights for free
    emitFragment(x, y, bary);
  }
}

Three signs decide coverage, and the top-left rule makes sure a shared edge is drawn exactly once.

Blending values across a triangle with the screen-space weights is wrong for anything in 3D. Far parts of a surface are squashed on screen, so equal steps across the pixels are not equal steps across the surface. The fix is to divide by w before blending and undo it after. Hardware does this for you; you only see the bug in software rasterizers and PlayStation 1 games.

What you're seeing

A courtyard of light and dark flagstones running away from the camera, with the knight standing at its far edge and the farmhouse beyond. The floor is one flat quad made of two triangles, and the dashed line is the seam between them. With affine blending the flagstones kink at the seam and the far rows come out too tall, so the floor looks folded.

Interpolation

The real version

Screen-space barycentrics are linear in the pixel plane. A texture coordinate is linear on the surface out in the world, and the divide by w bends the relationship between the two. What stays linear on screen is attr/w and 1/w, so the rasterizer blends those two and divides at the end.

Hardware does this for every interpolated value, called a varying, that the vertex shader hands to the pixel shader. That is why the bug only shows up when someone writes their own rasterizer.

In words: blend u divided by w and 1 divided by w across the triangle, then divide one by the other.

u_affine = λ0·u0 + λ1·u1 + λ2·u2 // wrong under perspective u_correct = (λ0·u0/w0 + λ1·u1/w1 + λ2·u2/w2) / (λ0/w0 + λ1/w1 + λ2/w2)

u0 to u2 are the texture coordinates at the corners, w0 to w2 are their clip-space w values, and λ0 to λ2 are the screen-space blend weights from the block above.

Anything you blend across a triangle in 3D gets divided by w first and multiplied back afterwards.

The depth buffer and z-fighting

A banner hangs 5 mm in front of a castle wall 30 m away. Change the depth format and the near plane to see whether the wall or the banner wins each pixel.

hover a pixel

The depth buffer is one number per pixel saying how far away the nearest thing drawn so far is. Each new fragment compares against it and is thrown away if something nearer is already there. The stored number is not metres: it is squashed so that most of its steps land close to the camera. Two surfaces closer together than one step at their distance round to the same value, and the result is the flickering stripes called z-fighting.

What you're seeing

A 64×64 patch of the depth buffer drawn as a heatmap. The castle wall is plane A, drawn first in amber; the banner is plane B, drawn second in blue, a few millimetres in front of or behind the stone. Brightness is the stored value, colour is which surface owns the pixel. The dark strip on the right is sky: nothing was drawn there, so the buffer still holds its cleared far value.

Where the banner covers the wall, stripes mean the buffer could not tell them apart and draw order decided. The "player's view" inset shows the result: stone showing through the banner in bands. The four tiles show the step size at the wall's distance for each format.

The real version

A fragment compares its own depth with the stored one. If the test fails it is discarded; if it passes, its depth replaces the stored value. With the standard projection near maps to 0 and far to 1, so the LESS test keeps the nearest surface. GREATER keeps the farthest, which is the wrong picture here and the right one for reversed-Z, where the projection maps near to 1 instead.

The stored value is z/w, not metres. How fast it changes with real depth falls off as 1/z², so a 24-bit buffer that resolves under a micrometre at the near plane resolves about half a millimetre at 30 m and 5 cm at 300 m. Two surfaces closer together than that step quantize, meaning round, to the same integer.

Which one wins is then decided by draw order and the tie rule, pixel by pixel, in stripes that follow the depth gradient. That pattern is z-fighting. Halving the near plane doubles the step everywhere, which is why the near plane matters more than the bit count.

Reversed-Z stores 1 − z as a 32-bit float and tests GREATER. A float has finer steps near zero, and reversing puts the far scene there, so the float's changing step size cancels the 1/z² fall-off.

In words: the stored value is a squashed depth, its step size grows with the square of the distance, and reversing the range lets a float absorb that growth.

z_buf = (A · z + B) / (−z) // z = z_view; D3D range [0,1]: A = far / (far − near) // B = −far · near / (far − near) dz_buf/dz = far·near / ((far − near) · z²) step(z) ≈ 2^(−bits) · z² · (far − near) / (far · near) reversed-Z: store 1 − z_buf as float32, test GREATER // the float exponent counters the 1/z² fall-off

z is view-space depth in metres, near and far are the camera planes, A and B are the projection constants, bits is the buffer's bit count, and step is the smallest depth difference the buffer can tell apart at that distance.

What this code does: reads the stored depth, rounds the fragment's depth to the buffer's precision, compares, discards on failure and writes on success.

// per fragment, fixed-function; runs before the shader when early-Z is possible
uint stored = depthBuffer[px];
uint zq     = quantize(fragment.z, bits);       // z already divided by w
bool pass   = (func == LESS) ? zq < stored : zq > stored;
if (!pass) discard;                              // equal depth fails LESS: the first drawn stays
depthBuffer[px] = zq;                            // unless depth writes are off (transparents)

The depth buffer stores z/w, its step grows with the square of the distance, and the near plane sets the scale for all of it.

Early-Z: skipping hidden pixels

Five trees stand in front of each other at the edge of the camp. Drag them and change the draw order to see how many times each pixel gets shaded.

Every pixel the shader colours that ends up hidden behind something else is wasted work. Overdraw is how many times a pixel gets drawn in one frame. Early-Z is the hardware trick of running the depth test before the shader instead of after, so a hidden fragment is dropped before it costs anything. It only helps if something nearer is already in the buffer, so draw order matters.

What you're seeing

Five trees on a 64×36 grid, drawn as a heatmap of how many times the shader ran on each pixel. Dark is zero, blue is once, amber and white are three or more. The white outlines are the trees' shapes, the nearest one biggest and lowest on screen, and the label next to each handle is its depth and its place in the draw order. The inset in the corner is the forest as the player sees it, which looks the same whatever the order.

The tiles scale the shaded count up to a 1920×1080 frame, in millions of fragments.

Try this

  • Untick the early-Z box. Every fragment is shaded whatever the order, and the count matches the early-Z off tile.
  • Drag the big near tree away so it no longer overlaps the others. The overdraw number in the caption falls.

The real version

In the pipeline diagram the depth test sits after the fragment shader. The hardware runs it before the shader whenever it can prove the answer would be the same. That is early-Z.

It is only legal when the shader does not write depth and does not discard, which means throw away its own fragment, because either one changes what the test would see. Alpha-tested foliage loses early-Z for exactly this reason and is a classic overdraw hot spot.

Front-to-back sorting makes later fragments fail cheaply; back-to-front shades every layer. Engines that cannot sort well use a depth prepass: draw everything once with an empty shader to fill the depth buffer, then draw again with the real shader and an EQUAL test, so each pixel is shaded exactly once.

In words: the cost is the shaded fragments times the shader cost, plus every rasterized fragment times the much smaller test cost.

shaded = Σ over draws of pixels passing early-Z overdraw = rasterized / pixels cost ≈ shaded · shader_cost + rasterized · ztest_cost

shaded is the fragments that passed the test before shading, rasterized is every fragment the rasterizer produced, pixels is the screen size, and the two cost terms are the price of shading or depth-testing one fragment.

What this code does: pass one fills depth with colour writes off, pass two shades with an EQUAL test so only the visible fragment per pixel runs the real shader.

// depth prepass: pass 1 fills the buffer with the cheapest possible shader
setDepthTest(LESS);  setDepthWrite(true);  setColorWrite(false);
drawOpaque(nullPixelShader);
// pass 2: only the visible fragment per pixel passes EQUAL, so it shades once
setDepthTest(EQUAL); setDepthWrite(false); setColorWrite(true);
drawOpaque(fullPixelShader);
// forcing early-Z on a shader that discards: HLSL [earlydepthstencil], GLSL layout(early_fragment_tests)

Draw near things first and keep discard out of opaque shaders, and each pixel gets shaded about once.

Testing tiles before pixels

The tent canvas again, now a big triangle on a 64×64 patch of the screen. Drag its corners and change the tile size to see how many per-pixel tests the GPU skips.

A big triangle covers thousands of pixels, and testing each one is slow. The GPU first checks whole tiles of pixels at once: a tile fully outside the triangle is thrown out, a tile fully inside is accepted whole, and only tiles the edges cross go on to per-pixel tests. This is why big triangles are cheap per pixel and tiny ones are not.

What you're seeing

A 64×64 pixel patch of the screen with the tent canvas across it, and the knight and campfire faint behind. Faint red tiles were thrown out after four tests per edge. Solid amber tiles were accepted whole, with no per-pixel tests. Blue-outlined tiles are the ones a tent edge crosses; inside them every pixel center is tested and covered centers show as amber squares.

The dashed box is the triangle's bounding box, the smallest rectangle around it. The tiles compare the work done at each level.

The real version

An edge function is linear, so over a rectangle its smallest and largest values sit at the corners. Four evaluations per edge tell you everything. If any edge is negative at all four corners, the tile cannot touch the triangle and is rejected. If all three edges are positive at all four corners, every pixel in the tile is covered and no per-pixel test is needed.

Hardware does this in two or three levels: a coarse rasterizer over 8×8 or 16×16 tiles, then a fine rasterizer over 2×2 blocks called quads. A triangle smaller than a tile still pays for the tile test and the quad test, and usually shades all four pixels of a quad to get derivatives, the per-pixel rate of change that texture filtering needs.

In words: reject when any edge's maximum over the corners is negative, accept when every edge's minimum is non-negative, otherwise test the pixels.

for edge i, tile corners c0..c3: reject if max(Ei(c0..c3)) < 0 for any i accept if min(Ei(c0..c3)) ≥ 0 for all i else test the T×T pixel centers

Ei is edge function i, c0 to c3 are the four corners of the tile, and T is the tile size in pixels.

What this code does: for each tile in the bounding box, finds each edge's min and max at the corners, then rejects, accepts whole, or hands the tile to the per-pixel loop.

// coarse stage: tiles inside the triangle's bounding box only
for (tile in bboxTiles) {
  bool out = false, full = true;
  for (int i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
    float lo = min4(E[i] at corners), hi = max4(E[i] at corners);
    out  |= hi < 0.0;                    // trivial reject
    full &= lo >= 0.0;                   // trivial accept
  }
  if (out) continue;
  if (full) emitAll(tile);             // no per-pixel edge tests
  else fineRasterize(tile);            // the loop from the coverage section
}

Four corner tests per edge let the GPU skip whole tiles, so per-pixel work only happens where an edge runs through.

Tradeoffs: which depth format

Memory is bytes per pixel times pixel count, 1 MB = 10⁶ bytes. Step is the smallest depth difference the buffer can tell apart at 30 m with near 0.1 m and far 500 m, the same formula the depth demo uses.

You are choosing between memory, precision, and whether you get a stencil, an extra 8-bit mask stored next to each depth value. Read the Step column first: smaller is better, and the two highlighted cells are the worst and the best on the list.

The D24 and D32F rows tie because a float between 0.5 and 1 has the same 2⁻²⁴ spacing as a 24-bit integer. A float only pays off when the values you care about sit near zero, which is exactly what reversing the range does: the far scene lands near 0, where a float has spare exponent bits. Multisampled targets multiply every number above by the sample count, so a 4×MSAA D32F at 4K is 133 MB before compression.

One naming trap: "Hi-Z", "early-Z" and "the depth prepass" are three different things, even though all three throw away hidden work. Early-Z is the per-fragment test moved before the shader. Hi-Z, short for hierarchical Z, is a coarse per-tile depth range the rasterizer keeps so it can reject whole tiles before any fragments exist, and the depth prepass is the strategy that fills the buffer so both of the others have something to reject against.
