HDR, Tone Mapping & Color

Tone mapping is how a game squeezes a scene with a blinding sun and a deep shadow onto a screen that can only show a thin slice of that range. Get it wrong and the sky turns into a white sheet, the shadows into black mud, and smooth gradients into visible bands. The thing people get wrong: gamma is not an old-monitor bug to switch off. It is how 8 bits get spent where your eye can see them, and the same buffer without it bands in every shadow.

Real light vs stored numbers

Hover either picture to see a linear value and the byte that stores it.

step back until the pattern blurs
dashed: pure gamma 2.2
Blend space

Your GPU does its lighting math in linear light, where a number twice as big means twice as many photons. Your screen and your 8-bit textures store a different number, an encoded value where equal steps look equal to your eye. Mix the two up and 50 % gray comes out too dark, and every blend between two colours goes muddy.

What you're seeing

Left picture, top row: a checkerboard of pure black and pure white pixels, then two flat gray patches. The checkerboard is 50 % light by construction, since half its pixels are on. Step back and only the right patch (byte 188) matches it; the middle patch (byte 128) is what you get by writing the linear number straight to the screen.

Below that are two ramps from black to white, and a red-to-green blend done two ways. The right picture is the sRGB curve: amber turns linear into encoded, blue turns encoded back into linear.

Try this

  • Drag Linear gray to 0.25. The checkerboard and the right patch stay matched; the middle patch goes much darker than both.
  • Click sRGB only, then linear only. The middle of the strip is a dim olive in one and a clean yellow in the other.
  • Hover the curve near the middle. 0.5 linear encodes to 0.735 (byte 188), and byte 128 decodes to only 0.216 linear.

The real version

The sRGB curve has two halves. The OETF, the opto-electronic transfer function, turns linear light into the encoded value a display stores. The EOTF goes the other way. Both are close to a power of 2.4 with a short straight piece near black; the dashed line is a plain gamma 2.2 for comparison.

Half of all linear light sits above byte 188, in the top quarter of the encoded range. That is why the lower ramp, which spaces linear values evenly, looks mostly bright.

The red-to-green strip is the bug you will actually ship. Lerp the encoded bytes (blend them by a straight fraction) and the midpoint is (128, 128, 0), a dim olive. Lerp in linear and encode after, and the midpoint is (188, 188, 0), the yellow you expected.

Texture filtering, mipmaps (the pre-shrunk copies of a texture), alpha blending (drawing see-through things) and MSAA resolve (averaging anti-aliasing samples) all hit this bug. That is why GPUs have _SRGB texture formats: the hardware decodes to linear before filtering and encodes after blending.

In words: below a tiny threshold the curve is a straight line; above it, take the 1/2.4 power, scale by 1.055 and subtract 0.055. Decoding runs the same steps backwards.

sRGB OETF (linear → encoded), c in 0..1: c ≤ 0.0031308 : e = 12.92 · c else : e = 1.055 · c^(1/2.4) − 0.055 sRGB EOTF (encoded → linear): e ≤ 0.04045 : c = e / 12.92 else : c = ((e + 0.055) / 1.055)^2.4 OETF(0.5) = 0.7354 → byte 188 EOTF(128/255) = 0.2159

c is the linear value and e the encoded value, both in 0..1. The constants 0.0031308 and 0.04045 are the same knee point seen from each side.

What this code does: encodes a linear colour to sRGB in one function, then blends two textures the right way: decode both, mix, encode once.

// resolve pass: shade in linear, encode once at the end
vec3 linearToSrgb(vec3 c) {
  vec3 lo = c * 12.92;
  vec3 hi = 1.055 * pow(c, vec3(1.0 / 2.4)) - 0.055;
  return mix(lo, hi, step(vec3(0.0031308), c));
}
// blending in linear: decode, lerp, encode
vec3 a = srgbToLinear(texA), b = srgbToLinear(texB);
vec3 outColor = linearToSrgb(mix(a, b, t));
// or let the hardware do it: bind an *_SRGB view and write linear

Do the math in linear, store and display in encoded, and convert exactly once at each boundary.

Exposure: picking the slice to show

Slide the exposure to choose which part of a 13-stop scene lands on the screen, or let auto exposure choose for you.

A real outdoor scene has a sun about 10 000 times brighter than the shadows. A screen can show roughly 8 stops, about 250 to 1, before the bottom turns to black noise. Exposure is the slider that picks which slice of that huge range gets shown: everything above the slice goes white, everything below goes black.

What you're seeing

The picture is a small 256×144 scene stored in nits, from 1 in the shadow to 10 000 in the sun. Under it is a histogram, a bar chart counting how many pixels sit at each brightness on a doubling scale. The amber line is the brightness that lands exactly on white; everything to the right of it is clipped, and the caption counts how much.

The real version

Exposure scales the scene by 2^EV before the display clips at 1.0. EV is exposure value, a count of stops: each +1 doubles the light. The scene has five stops between the shadow and the ground, three more to the sky, and another three to the sun. That is about 13 stops, 1 : 10 000, and a display shows roughly 8, so any single exposure throws away most of the range.

Auto exposure picks EV from the image. The usual rule (Reinhard 2002) puts the geometric mean of luminance, the average taken on the log scale, at middle gray, 0.18. Luminance is a pixel's brightness with red, green and blue weighted the way the eye weights them.

Games compute that mean from a luminance histogram on the GPU and discard the darkest and brightest few percent, so the sun does not drag the average. The eye adapts slowly, so the value is filtered over time. The Adapt time slider sets the time constant, the time it takes to close most of the gap to the target.

In words: scale by 2 to the EV, take each pixel's luminance, average the logs, then aim the exposure so that average lands on 0.18 and ease toward it over time.

exposed = L_nits · 2^EV Y = 0.2126 R + 0.7152 G + 0.0722 B L̄ = exp( mean( log(δ + Y) ) ) δ = 1e-4 EV_target = log2( 0.18 / L̄ ) EV += (EV_target − EV) · (1 − e^(−dt/τ))

L_nits is scene brightness, Y is luminance, L̄ is the log-average, δ is a tiny floor so log(0) never happens, τ is the adapt time and dt is the frame time.

What this code does: pass one drops every pixel into one of 64 brightness bins; pass two averages the middle bins, skips the extremes, and eases the exposure toward the target.

// compute pass 1: 64-bin histogram of log2 luminance, one atomicAdd per pixel
float lum = dot(hdr.rgb, vec3(0.2126, 0.7152, 0.0722));
float t = (log2(max(lum, 1e-4)) - minLog2) / (maxLog2 - minLog2);
atomicAdd(bins[uint(clamp(t, 0.0, 1.0) * 63.0)], 1u);
// compute pass 2: weighted mean, skipping the bottom 5 % and top 2 % of pixels
float sum = 0.0, n = 0.0;
for (int i = lowBin; i < highBin; i++) { sum += binCenter(i) * bins[i]; n += bins[i]; }
float avgLog2 = sum / n;
float evTarget = log2(0.18) - avgLog2;      // key value 0.18 on middle gray
ev = mix(evTarget, evPrev, exp(-dt / tau)); // temporal adaptation

Exposure picks an 8-stop slice of a 13-stop scene; auto exposure aims the log-average brightness at middle gray and eases there over time.

Tone curves: the soft squeeze

Clipping is brutal: every value above white becomes the same flat white. A tone curve is a softer squeeze. It leaves the mid-tones mostly alone and bends the bright end gently toward white instead of slamming into it. The shape of that bend is most of what people mean by a game's "look".

What you're seeing

Left: the five curves, with input brightness along the bottom on a doubling scale and output up the side. The shaded areas mark the toe (the shadow end) and the shoulder (the highlight end).

Right: the scene through the chosen curve. The strip along the bottom is one pure red at rising intensity, ×0.25 up to ×64. It is the test for how a curve treats colour as it gets brighter.

Try this

  • Click Reinhard, then ACES fit. Reinhard looks flat and gray; the S shape of ACES puts the contrast back.
  • Switch Apply to from per channel to luminance. Per channel, the red strip goes red, then pink, then white. On luminance it stays red until it clips.
  • Pick Reinhard ext. and drag White point W from 4 to 16. The curve needs a brighter input to reach white, so the highlights flatten out.

The real version

A tone curve is a function from scene-linear (the light in the scene) to display-linear (the light the screen will make), applied before the sRGB encoding from the first section. It goes through 0.18 at about the middle and approaches 1.0 without ever getting there. The toe decides what happens to shadows, the shoulder how gently highlights roll off.

Plain Reinhard has no toe and a very long shoulder: 0.5 maps to 0.33, and it needs a scene value of 9 to reach 0.9, so everything looks flat. The extended version adds a white point W, the input that maps to exactly 1.0, and that restores contrast. Hable's curve from Uncharted 2 and the ACES fit both have an S shape, which is what "filmic" means in practice.

Where you apply the curve matters as much as its shape. Per channel, each of R, G and B saturates on its own, so a bright red gains green and blue and turns white, the way film does. Applied to luminance only, the hue is kept and a bright red stays red until it clips, which reads as a flat neon patch.

ACES per channel is the Unreal default. Luminance-only Reinhard is what most people mean when they say "Reinhard looks washed out".

The AgX curve here is the polynomial sigmoid (an S-shaped polynomial) fit in log2 space, without the inset and outset matrices the full transform uses. Those matrices rotate the primaries, the base red, green and blue, inward before the curve. That is what gives the full version its clean highlight desaturation.

In words: each curve takes one input brightness x and returns an output y in 0..1. Luminance mode runs the curve on brightness alone, then scales the colour to match.

Reinhard y = x / (1 + x) Reinhard ext. y = x (1 + x/W²) / (1 + x) Hable H(x) = ((x(Ax+CB)+DE) / (x(Ax+B)+DF)) − E/F y = H(2x) / H(W) A=.15 B=.50 C=.10 D=.20 E=.02 F=.30 W=11.2 ACES (Narkowicz 2015) y = x(2.51x + 0.03) / (x(2.43x + 0.59) + 0.14) AgX-like t = (log2 x + 12.474) / 16.5, then a 6th-order sigmoid in t luminance mode y_rgb = rgb · T(Y) / Y

x is the scene-linear input, y the display-linear output, W the white point, Y the luminance and T the chosen curve. A to F are Hable's six shape constants.

What this code does: exposes the HDR colour, runs the ACES fit either on each channel or on luminance only, then sRGB-encodes the result.

// tone-map fragment shader: HDR RGBA16F in, sRGB8 out
vec3 hdr = texture(sceneHdr, uv).rgb * exp2(ev);
vec3 aces(vec3 x) {
  return clamp((x * (2.51 * x + 0.03)) / (x * (2.43 * x + 0.59) + 0.14), 0.0, 1.0);
}
vec3 mapped;
if (perChannel) mapped = aces(hdr);           // highlights desaturate
else {
  float y = dot(hdr, vec3(0.2126, 0.7152, 0.0722));
  mapped = hdr * (aces(vec3(y)).x / max(y, 1e-5)); // hue kept, may clip
}
fragColor = vec4(linearToSrgb(mapped), 1.0);     // or write to an _SRGB target

A tone curve trades a hard clip for a soft roll-off, and running it per channel is what makes bright colours fade to white like film.

Why 8 bits band, and the fix

Compare one dark gradient stored four ways, and switch the dither on and off.

hover a strip for its stored value

A gradient stored with too few steps shows visible bands, a staircase where you wanted a ramp. Storing 8-bit values in linear wastes almost every step on the bright end; the sRGB encoding moves them to where your eye can see them. Dithering hides the rest: a sprinkle of noise before rounding turns bands into fine grain.

What you're seeing

The real version

The gradient runs from black to a linear value of top. An 8-bit linear target, a buffer with 256 values per channel spaced evenly in linear light, has 255 steps between 0 and 1. The dark end gets almost none of them: the whole range below 1 % of white is two codes.

An 8-bit sRGB target spends its codes where the eye can see them, and the same 1 % gets 25 codes. That is the entire reason the encoding exists, and why "linear render target" and "8-bit" do not belong in the same sentence.

Ten bits give four times the codes, enough that the steps are below the eye's threshold in most of the range. Dithering fixes banding at any depth by adding noise of about one code before rounding. The average is then exact and the error becomes fine grain.

Triangular noise (two uniform random samples summed) keeps the noise variance, its average strength, independent of the signal, so gradients do not show a texture change at the step boundaries. Blue noise or interleaved gradient noise puts the grain in high frequencies, fine and fast-changing, where it is least visible.

In words: one step is 1 over the number of codes. Then count how many codes sit below 1 % of white in each format.

step = 1 / (2^bits − 1) 8-bit 0.00392 · 10-bit 0.000978 codes below linear 0.01: 8-bit linear floor(0.01 · 255) = 2 8-bit sRGB floor(OETF(0.01) · 255) = 25 10-bit sRGB floor(OETF(0.01) · 1023) = 102

bits is the depth of the buffer, OETF is the sRGB encode from the first section, and floor rounds down to a whole code.

What this code does: makes a cheap per-pixel noise, shapes it into a triangle, adds it to the encoded colour and rounds to the nearest of 255 codes.

// dithered quantization at the end of the tone-map pass
float ign(vec2 p) {                        // interleaved gradient noise, Jimenez 2014
  return fract(52.9829189 * fract(dot(p, vec2(0.06711056, 0.00583715))));
}
float u1 = ign(gl_FragCoord.xy), u2 = ign(gl_FragCoord.xy + 17.0);
float tri = u1 + u2 - 1.0;                  // triangular in [-1, 1]
float codes = 255.0;                        // 1023.0 for a 10-bit swapchain
vec3 e = linearToSrgb(mapped);              // dither in the encoded domain
fragColor.rgb = floor(e * codes + 0.5 + tri) / codes;

Store 8-bit data with the sRGB curve and dither before rounding; linear 8-bit will band no matter what you do.

Every screen can show only a triangle of colours out of everything your eye can see. A colour outside that triangle has to be clipped or pulled inward, and the two choices look different. HDR output is a separate problem: a new encoding, PQ, where each code means a fixed brightness in nits, so the game has to decide how bright "white" is.

What you're seeing

Left: the CIE 1931 horseshoe, the map of every colour a human can see, with three display triangles on it: sRGB, P3 and Rec.2020. The white handle is the colour in the table above. Each row is that colour written in one space; a channel outside 0..1 means that screen cannot show it, and the two swatches show the clipped and the gamut-mapped fix.

Right: the two curves a game writes to the screen buffer, plotted against real nits. Blue is sRGB for normal (SDR) output, amber is PQ for HDR10.

  • Compare the two swatches under the handle. The clipped one has shifted hue and looks brighter; the mapped one keeps the hue and loses some saturation.
  • Drag SDR white in HDR mode from 200 to 400 nits. The blue sRGB curve slides right: UI white now lands higher up the PQ curve.

The real version

A color space is three primaries (its purest red, green and blue) and a white point. The triangle they span is the gamut, everything the display can show. Rec.709 and sRGB share primaries and cover about 35 % of the visible horseshoe. P3 pushes red and green out. Rec.2020 goes almost to the spectral locus, the curved edge of pure single-wavelength colours, where no real display reaches.

Drag the handle outside the sRGB triangle and one of the sRGB channels goes negative. Clipping it to 0 shifts the hue and brightens the swatch. Gamut mapping instead pulls the color toward white along a straight line until it fits, which keeps the hue and loses saturation.

Between spaces you multiply by a 3×3 matrix built from the primaries: RGB → XYZ for the source, XYZ → RGB for the destination. XYZ is the device-independent middle space the horseshoe is drawn from. The Rec.709 to Rec.2020 matrix below is computed from the primaries on this page and matches the published one to four digits.

A wide-gamut pipeline renders in one working space, usually sRGB-linear or ACEScg, and converts once at output.

The HDR output path replaces the sRGB OETF with PQ. PQ is absolute: code 0.5 always means about 92 nits, code 1.0 means 10 000. So the game has to decide how many nits its UI white is (the slider), scale the scene to match, and tone-map to the display's peak, which it reads from the EDID, the display's self-description, often 600 to 1 000 nits.

Then it converts to Rec.2020 primaries and encodes into a 10-bit swapchain, the buffer handed to the screen. Dolby Vision on consoles adds per-frame metadata on top of the same PQ signal.

In words: PQ divides nits by 10 000, then bends that through two fixed fractions and a power so the codes follow how the eye sees brightness. The matrix converts a Rec.709 colour to Rec.2020.

PQ (ST.2084) OETF, Y = nits / 10 000: m1 = 2610/16384 = 0.1593 m2 = 2523/4096 · 128 = 78.84 c1 = 3424/4096 = 0.8359 c2 = 2413/4096 · 32 = 18.85 c3 = 2392/4096 · 32 = 18.69 E = ( (c1 + c2 · Y^m1) / (1 + c3 · Y^m1) )^m2 Rec.709 → Rec.2020 (computed from primaries):

Y is nits over 10 000, E is the encoded PQ value in 0..1, and m1, m2, c1, c2 and c3 are the fixed constants from the ST.2084 standard.

What this code does: tone-maps, scales white to the chosen nits, changes primaries to Rec.2020, then PQ-encodes into a 10-bit buffer.

// HDR10 output: scene-linear Rec.709 → tone map → Rec.2020 → PQ, 10-bit swapchain
vec3 c = tonemap(sceneLinear * exp2(ev));       // display-linear, 1.0 = paper white
c *= paperWhiteNits;                             // e.g. 200 nits, from the user's HDR slider
c = rec709ToRec2020 * c;                         // primaries change, still linear
vec3 y = clamp(c / 10000.0, 0.0, 1.0);
vec3 ym = pow(y, vec3(0.1593017578125));
fragColor.rgb = pow((0.8359375 + 18.8515625 * ym) / (1.0 + 18.6875 * ym), vec3(78.84375));
// swapchain: R10G10B10A2_UNORM with the ST2084 color space flag

A gamut is the triangle a screen can show; HDR10 output means Rec.2020 primaries, PQ encoding, and a paper-white brightness the game has to choose.

Undoing the curve matters in one place. TAA (temporal anti-aliasing, which blends each frame with the last few) and UI compositing want to work in tone-mapped space, so one blinding pixel does not dominate the blend, and then undo the curve afterwards. Reinhard and the ACES fit have exact inverses; Hable and AgX do not, so engines that use them keep a cheap Reinhard pair around for that step.

The naming trap is "gamma correction". It sounds like fixing a defect in old CRT monitors, so people conclude a modern pipeline should just work in linear 8-bit. The CRT's curve was an accident, but it lined up with how the eye sees contrast, and sRGB kept it on purpose: store 8-bit data encoded, do the math in linear, and remove the curve only from your shading, never from your buffers.
